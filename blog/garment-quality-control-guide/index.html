Garment quality control: what should a buyer check?

By Huimin TangPublished 17 November 202612 min read

Garment quality control checks clothing against agreed requirements for materials, measurements, construction, performance, labels, and packing. For a buyer, the useful result is evidence that connects the approved specification to inspection findings and a shipment decision. QIMA’s garment inspection procedures describe these checks.

At Tapa Works, we manufacture aloha shirts in our own Guangzhou factory and check fabric on arrival, cutting, sewing, and finished garments. Use this guide to read a report, question an improvement chart, and work through a failed checklist item. For our inspection scope, see our Quality Control page.

What garment QC checks tell you about your order

Garment QC checks tell you whether the inspected materials and garments meet the requirements you agreed, which problems are still open, and what supports a decision to hold or release. A sampled pass supports acceptance under the agreed plan, but it doesn't guarantee a defect-free shipment. For more, see TradeAider's Improving Garment Quality Control: A Step-by-Step Guide.

Start with approved materials, construction, measurements, labels and a reference sample. During production, check the work against those approvals. Before dispatch, reconcile workmanship, performance evidence and packing. Keep the US-market labeling and any applicable test evidence alongside the inspection verdict. As QIMA's Garment Quality Control & Inspection Procedures explains, each of these checks answers a different question.

At Tapa Works, our final inspection covers 100% of garments and takes 2 to 3 days. For pre-shipment sampling, we use AQL 2.5 for major defects and 4.0 for minor defects, checked against your approved sample. Our Quality Control page sets out our inspection scope. Agree on the sampling plan and severity rules before anyone starts counting findings. An AQL setting doesn't give permission to ship a fixed percentage of faulty clothing.

Follow the report from finding to release

Our redacted Palm order record covers 500 adult cotton aloha shirts in one print, sizes S to XL, made to specification revision C. Its header identifies the fabric batch, the inspector, the selected cartons and the garment identifiers. We've removed the customer's branding but kept the links between the inspected goods and their records.

On October 12, 2026, the independent inspection found open underarm seams, and the lot went on hold. The close-up of garment C-A showed an opening that failed the major-defect criterion in reference sheet D. Lin, our QC lead, authorized containment. Mei, our sewing supervisor, took responsibility for sorting the held goods and repairing the affected joins.

The closure file links C-A's original photograph to its repair record. A fresh inspection on October 15 found no open seams or other counted defects in the selected garments. That same day, the buyer's quality manager reviewed the repeat report, reconciled the label, packing and test documents, and signed the release.

Read that chain for what each record actually proves. The specification defines the target, the original photo supports the finding, and the hold identifies the goods waiting on a decision. A repair photo shows the visible correction on that garment. The repeat report establishes the scope of verification, and the release signature shows who accepted the order. When a report just says "fixed," ask for the full chain.

Settle borderline grades before they change the verdict

Palm's buyer first graded a loose thread inside the hem as major, while our inspector graded it minor. They settled on minor because the seam stayed secure and the thread was hidden during wear. Both graded the open underarm seam as major, because arm movement exposed the opening and put stress on the join. Neither finding was safety-critical. Reference sheet D keeps the original close-ups, the initial grades, the reasons and the final grades the buyer approved.

When a grade is disputed, talk it through in terms of location, visibility, function and safety. A thread end inside a secure hem and stitching coming apart at a stressed join call for different decisions. Tetra's Quality Control in Clothing guide describes how position and functional effect shape classification. Keep the agreed criterion with the original garment photos so the next inspector can apply it the same way.

Separate a measurement pass from fit approval

Correct flat measurements don't guarantee an acceptable fit, and QIMA's Garment Quality Control & Inspection Procedures describes fit as a separate check. Settle fit during development, before you approve bulk. Our Sampling page covers that stage.

During Palm development, the medium sample measured 56.0 cm across the chest and 74.0 cm in body length, which matched its targets within the specified ±1 cm tolerance. Even so, the front and side fit photos showed the placket pulling when the wearer reached forward. After three separate 40°C home washes with line drying, the readings were 55.0 cm and 72.5 cm. Shrinkage stayed within our 3% limit, but the movement problem hadn't gone away.

Our pattern team redistributed the back-shoulder ease and adjusted the sleeve pitch while keeping the approved chest and length targets. Repeat photos of the medium and large samples showed the placket lying flat in the same reach test, and the buyer approved revision C. That result applies only to the tested sizes and that cotton style.

Read costs and delays against the order scope

You book and pay the independent inspection agency directly, and you give us at least 3 business days’ notice. If goods fail because of our workmanship, we rework them and pay the reinspection fee. Laboratory testing costs extra unless your quote lists it. You pay the lab's rate per test item, and nothing is booked until you approve it. Our Quality Control page has the details.

Our own records give the measured version for a repeat of the same Palm style in November 2026. The same underarm fault turned up at the inline sewing check on day 9 of production: 18 shirts from one operator's bundle were held, sorted and repaired in 40 minutes of factory labor, and the final inspection and the buyer's single independent visit went ahead on the original dates. On the October order, the fault was found only at the independent inspection, so all 500 shirts were sorted, a second visit was needed and release moved by 3 days.

Ask for the approved criteria, the completed report, any unresolved exceptions and the authorized disposition all together. The tools and worked checklist below help you check that the findings, corrections and release decision connect.

What are the 7 QC tools used in apparel?

A common apparel QC toolkit includes check sheets, Pareto charts, fishbone diagrams, histograms, control charts, scatter diagrams and flowcharts. The Scribd apparel-tool summary lists this combination. Lists vary, though, and some versions use stratification in place of flowcharts.

Manufacturing Connected's quality-tool review explains the shared tools but names graphs as its final tool. In practice, what matters is which decision each tool supports. A check sheet records what you observe. A checklist defines what to inspect.

ToolJobApparel application
Check sheetCapture observations consistentlyRecord seam faults by garment and line
Pareto chartRank categoriesCompare recurring fault counts or repair burden
Fishbone diagramOrganize possible causesSeparate machine, material, and sewing-method hypotheses
HistogramShow a distributionExamine chest measurements within the same size
Control chartTrack process variation over timeMonitor repeated samples using statistical limits
Scatter diagramExplore paired variablesCompare sewing speed with seam-opening results
FlowchartMap work and checkpointsLocate where seam checks occur before packing

Follow a cause through a trial

In Palm's repair investigation, the raw first-pass rows recorded an open underarm seam on C-A, C-B, C-C and C-D. C-A also had a loose thread, and C-E had only a loose thread. Grouping showed that the seam findings all came from the same underarm operation. Lin's hypothesis log listed missed backtacking, thread tension and fabric damage. A trial kept fabric, operator and thread the same while correcting the backtack method, and the seam stayed closed in the agreed handling check. Mei then retrained the operation and checked the repaired joins.

For a comparable first-pass follow-up, Lin checked 50 new Palm shirts from the same line and size mix on October 16, 2026. She found 1 open seam, compared with 4 among 50 at the initial inspection. Repaired garments were left out of that comparison. The lower count supported continuing to monitor the method. It didn't prove the process would stay stable over the long term.

Here, the check sheet, the grouping and the cause log did the useful work. A histogram would answer a question about dimensions, and a scatter diagram would need paired measurements. Don't make a chart just to complete the toolkit.

Control limits describe how the process has actually behaved. Specification tolerances describe the result the buyer requires. A stable process can still miss the requirement. In the same way, an association in a scatter diagram is a hypothesis to test, not proof of cause. See Manufacturing Connected's control-chart and correlation discussion.

These assumed rows contain 5 defects on 3 defective garments, out of 5 units inspected. Defects per hundred units equals 5 ÷ 5 × 100 = 100. Percent defective equals 3 ÷ 5 × 100 = 60%. By frequency, loose threads come to 3, open seams to 1 and needle fragments to 1.

For the same assumed findings, allow 1 minute to trim each loose thread and 8 minutes to repair the open seam. That gives 3 minutes of thread rework and 8 minutes of seam rework in total. A needle fragment gets no routine repair estimate. Stop release, contain the affected goods and investigate the safety hazard.

Now suppose a later inspection finds 2 defective garments out of 2 inspected. The raw count falls from 3 to 2, but percent defective rises from 60% to 100%. If garment A passes after repair, keep that recheck in a separate record. Counting it as another clean first-pass garment would distort the denominator.

Before you interpret a change, group the results by style, size, line, fabric batch and inspection period. A bigger share of easy-to-sew styles can improve the overall rate while the troublesome style stays exactly where it was. Compare matched groups using the same criteria and counting rules.

A Pareto chart orders the observations you give it. It doesn't promise that problems will be concentrated in any particular way. Ask for containment when severity calls for it, investigation when repetition calls for it and evidence when a repair estimate drives spending. Before you accept a claim that things have improved, ask for the raw denominator, the counting definitions, the tested cause and a comparable follow-up.

How do I use a garment QC checklist?

Agree the specification, inspection method, sampling plan and acceptance rules before anyone checks a garment. Then record the actual results against each requirement. A failed row needs containment, correction, verification and an authorized release. TradeAider's staged QC guidance shows how those records connect.

Use the checklist for approvals before production, to catch drift during production and to check the finished lot before shipment. Record the revision, method, garment identity, result, evidence, disposition, owner and date. A tick on its own can't tell you what passed.

Make measurements repeatable

On Palm garment C-F, revision C specified a flat half-chest of 56.0 cm with a ±1 cm tolerance. On October 12, Lin read 57.1 cm and Mei read 56.9 cm. They checked both tapes against the same steel reference and found that Lin had pulled the side seam outward. They measured again with the buttons closed, the shirt flat, wrinkles smoothed without stretching and the tape on the approved underarm measurement line. This time both read 56.9 cm. The log keeps the original readings, the method correction, a positioning photo and the pass decision.

Don't average conflicting readings, and don't round a failure into tolerance. Sort out positioning and equipment first, then repeat the agreed method. For a stretch garment, state whether the measurement is taken relaxed or extended. Put the units next to the result, and keep the fit assessment separate from the dimensional verdict.

Carry selected garments into the finding ledger

Palm's signed plan used ISO 2859-1, normal single sampling, General Inspection Level II, code H, with 50 garments randomly selected from the 500-piece lot. Major defects used AQL 2.5, accepting at 3 and rejecting at 4. Minor defects used AQL 4.0, accepting at 5 and rejecting at 6. The plan counted defective garments within each severity class, and any critical finding required a hold.

For random selection to work, the inspector needs access to the whole defined lot. Record how cartons and positions were chosen, and map size coverage to the packing list. Don't let a supplier swap in a convenient stack of clean garments. If the assortment or the lot boundary changes, settle the plan before you continue.

C-A goes in both the major and minor columns because it had an open seam and a loose thread. C-B, C-C and C-D go in major only, and C-E goes in minor only. That reconciles 6 fault observations, 5 unique defective garments, 4 major-class garments and 2 minor-class garments. The major rejection threshold triggered the hold. Adding the class totals together would overstate the number of unique defective garments.

The synthetic tally earlier explains why those counts differ. A sampling decision has to use the counting basis written into its plan. Don't switch from defective-garment counts to defects per hundred units halfway through a report.

The grading agreement goes along with the checklist too. Use the signed reference sheet and original close-ups described earlier. If a disputed grade changes, keep a record of the initial grade, the final grade, the reason, the approver and the resulting lot decision. The buyer must be able to see any changed classification.

Mei's containment log shows that all 500 shirts were held. Sorting found 12 open underarm seams, including the sampled failures, and every affected join was repaired. After sorting and repair, the repeat inspection checked 50 freshly selected garments. The buyer signed the release after the repeat report and document reconciliation, 3 calendar days after the hold. The original failed inspection stayed in the file.

A repair photo supports the correction, but on its own it doesn't show that the shipment is ready. Ask for evidence of four things: the held goods stayed identifiable, the repair covered the affected scope, the verifier used the agreed criteria and the authorized person reviewed any unresolved exceptions.

Treat US-market documentation as a separate release requirement. Confirm that the approved content, care, origin and responsible-business wording matches the actual garment. Also confirm that the applicable test evidence covers the product and materials you're shipping. QIMA's inspection procedures discuss general garment checks and destination-market labeling.

We base care wording on fabric and print wash tests and send you the label artwork for approval. See our Custom Labels & Hang Tags page. Agree the packing reference as well. Our Packaging page describes the service available. A sampled workmanship pass can't settle an unresolved label, test or packing requirement.

Agree on the QC evidence before your next garment order

Before production starts, ask the factory to agree with you on the specification revision, the defect reference sheet and the sampling and counting rules. Agree who is responsible at each checkpoint and who has the authority to hold and release goods.

Then ask for a completed report and an example of a closed corrective action. Follow one garment's identity from selection through finding, repair, verification and release. If that link breaks anywhere in the record, ask for the missing evidence before you approve dispatch. To talk through the requirements for your style, visit our Request a Quote page.
